The idea, in one breath

Big, healthy companies sometimes have a bad week for no good reason. If the stock is still in a long-term uptrend and the overall market is fine, that dip often snaps back. Buy a small slice during the dip, sell when it recovers, put the cash back in the pool, repeat.

The engine watches 10 of the largest companies on the exchange, picked by size with no more than 2 per sector, so it's a rule and not my gut. Each buy is 20% of the pool. Even on a pool as small as $20, that would be $4 a trade, which works because the broker sells fractional shares with no commission.

The rules

Buy when all are true

  1. The stock is above its 200-day average (uptrend)
  2. It's below its lower Bollinger band (a real dip for that stock)
  3. The index fund is above its 200-day average (market is healthy)
  4. Cash is over 35% of the pool (reserve stays intact)
  5. I don't already own it

Sell when any is true

  • It's back above its 20-day average (the bounce happened)
  • It broke below its 200-day average (trend is dead)
  • It's up 6% from what I paid (take the win)
  • It's down 8% from what I paid (cut the loss)
All four sell automatically.

These are simple rules Claude came up with to test the proof of concept. They are not financial rules, a trading system or advice of any kind.

Why the automation lives in the broker

I built this with an AI assistant connected to my brokerage through an MCP connector (Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets AI assistants use other apps' tools; Anthropic's intro). It researched the strategy, had the rules reviewed by a second AI, and built every rule as a broker "flow". Then it stepped out of the loop.

Every extra thing between a signal and a trade is another thing that can break.

If an AI agent had to wake up, check prices and place orders, the engine would depend on my laptop being on, a session staying alive, an API not timing out, and the agent reading the market right. Broker flows run on the broker's own servers, right next to the price data. So the loop is short: the market moves, a rule checks it, an order goes out.

The AI also never placed a trade. It built everything as alerts, and I flipped the ones I wanted into real orders myself. That split felt right: the AI does the thinking and the building, and the human owns the button that spends money.

Questrade today, others tomorrow

This runs on Questrade, whose flows handle the conditions, the orders and the scheduling, with an MCP connector letting the AI set it up. Nothing here is specific to one broker. Any platform with rule-based order automation and fractional shares should be able to run the same loop, and Robinhood is next on my list to try.

Maintenance: almost none

Once a month I bump the buy size to 20% of whatever the pool is now, and glance at the flow history for anything that paused. It takes two minutes and could just as easily be a calendar reminder. The pool rebalances itself: sales flow back to cash, and the reserve rule stops it from going all in.

Try it with as little as $20

  1. Pick a broker with fractional shares, rule-based flows and low or no commission.
  2. Pick your list by a rule (size, sector caps), not by favourites.
  3. Build every rule as an alert first and watch what it would have done.
  4. Flip to real orders only when the alerts make sense to you.
  5. Keep a simple trade log. Taxes want it, and so will future you.

The honest scoreboard

Most professional funds don't beat a plain index fund, and quick dip-buying works least well on the biggest, most-watched stocks. So the bar is simple. After 30 trades, the engine has to beat the same money sitting in an index fund, with no bigger drawdowns. If it can't, the index fund wins and I'll say so here.
